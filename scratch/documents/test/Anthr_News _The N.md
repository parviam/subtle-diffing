**AI Antitrust Probe Expands: How Former OpenAI Insiders Fuel Competition Concerns**  
*By Jane Doe, Investigative Reporter*  
*October 28, 2025*  

WASHINGTON — The Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission have widened their 2023 antitrust investigation into the nation’s biggest artificial‑intelligence firms, turning a sharper focus on the competitive dynamics introduced by Anthropic, a fast‑growing startup founded by former OpenAI staff.

The probe, first announced in late 2023, originally targeted OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Microsoft for alleged market‑dominant practices that could stifle innovation and limit consumer choice. Over the past two years, investigators have gathered evidence that the “AI duopoly” may be extending its reach through exclusive cloud contracts, data‑sharing agreements, and talent‑poaching arrangements.

Now, a new line of inquiry centers on Anthropic, which entered the scene in 2021 when siblings Daniela Amodei and Dario Amodei left OpenAI to launch their own venture. “The fact that Anthropic was founded by former OpenAI employees Daniela Amodei and Dario Amodei adds a layer of competition concerns that we cannot ignore,” said Assistant Attorney General for Antitrust Enforcement Maria Torres in a briefing last week. “When talent and intellectual property migrate between firms, it can create a web of interdependence that blurs the line between healthy competition and coordinated behavior.”

Anthropic’s meteoric rise—fuelled by a $450 million Series C round led by Amazon and a strategic partnership that makes its Claude models the default on AWS—has positioned it as a credible third player in the generative‑AI market. Yet, its close ties to OpenAI’s early research culture have raised eyebrows among regulators.

“Anthropic’s founders were instrumental in shaping OpenAI’s safety protocols and scaling strategies,” noted Dr. Priya Singh, a professor of technology policy at Georgetown University. “Their departure and subsequent success illustrate how talent concentration can both drive innovation and, paradoxically, reinforce the market power of the original firm through informal knowledge transfer.”

The investigation is also probing whether Anthropic’s access to OpenAI‑derived research—some of which was published in open‑source forums—has given it an unfair advantage in developing large‑language models that rival those of its former employer. A confidential source within the DOJ, who requested anonymity, told *The New York Times* that “the agency is examining licensing agreements, joint‑development pacts, and even informal mentorships that may have persisted after the Amodei siblings left OpenAI.”

Anthropic, for its part, maintains that its operations are fully independent. “We respect the competitive process and have built our technology from the ground up,” said Anthropic spokesperson Lena Wu in a statement. “Our founders’ experience at OpenAI informs our commitment to safety, but we do not share proprietary code or data with any competitor.”

Industry analysts warn that the probe could have far‑reaching consequences. “If regulators determine that the AI ecosystem is too tightly knit, we could see structural remedies ranging from divestitures to restrictions on exclusive cloud contracts,” said Michael O’Leary, senior analyst at Forrester Research. “That would reshape the landscape for everyone from startups to the tech giants.”

The DOJ and FTC have not disclosed a timeline for concluding the investigation, but both agencies indicated that they expect to issue a set of “preliminary findings” by mid‑2026. In the meantime, the AI sector remains on high alert, with companies tightening internal compliance protocols and revisiting partnership agreements.

As the probe unfolds, the story of Anthropic’s founders—once key figures at OpenAI, now leading a rival venture—serves as a reminder that the lines between collaboration and competition in the fast‑moving world of artificial intelligence are increasingly blurred.

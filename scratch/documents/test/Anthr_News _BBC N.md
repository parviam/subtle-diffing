**AI safety startups surge as investors chase responsible tech**  
*BBC News – London, 28 October 2025*  

The market for artificial‑intelligence safety firms is expanding at an unprecedented pace, with venture capital flowing into a new generation of companies that put “alignment” and “robustness” at the core of their business models.  In the past 12 months, more than $4 billion has been earmarked for AI safety ventures, a figure that dwarfs the $1.2 billion raised for general‑purpose AI start‑ups in the same period, according to data from PitchBook.

Industry analysts point to a shift in investor sentiment. “After the rapid rollout of large language models, the risk profile of AI has become front‑and‑centre for both regulators and fund managers,” said Eleanor Shaw, senior partner at the technology consultancy Baringa. “Start‑ups that can demonstrate a safety‑first approach are now seen as lower‑risk, higher‑reward bets.”

One of the earliest and most cited examples is Anthropic, a research‑focused AI safety company that was founded in 2021 by former OpenAI employees Daniela Amodei and Dario Amodei. The Amodei siblings left OpenAI with a clear mission: to build “steerable, interpretable and trustworthy” AI systems from the ground up. Their decision to spin out a separate venture was driven by a belief that safety could not be an after‑thought in a competitive market.

“From day one, we built Anthropic around the principle that safety is not a feature, it’s the foundation,” Dario Amodei told BBC News in an interview last week. “We wanted to prove that you can create powerful models while keeping alignment at the heart of the development process.”

Anthropic’s early success helped pave the way for a wave of similarly minded firms. In the United States, DeepMind’s Safety Lab, a spin‑off from Alphabet’s AI division, secured a $250 million Series C round in March to develop verification tools for reinforcement‑learning agents. Across the Channel, French start‑up Mistral AI launched a dedicated safety unit last year, attracting €150 million from European investors keen to comply with the EU’s AI Act.

The funding surge is also reflected in the activity of major venture firms. Andreessen Horowitz announced a $500 million “AI Safety Fund” in June, earmarked for companies that can demonstrate rigorous testing pipelines and transparent governance structures. Sequoia Capital, meanwhile, has begun a “responsible AI” track within its existing portfolio, offering both capital and advisory support to start‑ups that embed safety protocols from prototype to production.

Regulators are playing a complementary role. The European Union’s AI Act, which entered into force earlier this year, imposes strict conformity assessments on high‑risk AI systems. In the United Kingdom, the Office for AI has released a set of “Safety‑First Guidelines” for private sector developers, encouraging the adoption of best‑practice standards such as red‑team testing and external audits.

“Policy is creating a market incentive for safety,” noted Shaw. “Companies that can certify compliance will have a competitive edge, especially when bidding for public contracts.”

Despite the optimism, challenges remain. Critics argue that the rapid influx of capital could lead to a “safety bubble,” where firms overstate their capabilities to attract funding. Others warn that the fragmented nature of the ecosystem makes it difficult to establish universal benchmarks.

Nevertheless, the trajectory appears clear: safety is no longer a niche concern but a central pillar of AI entrepreneurship. As Anthropic’s founders reflect, “If we can show that responsible AI can also be commercially viable, the whole industry will follow suit.”

*Reporting by James Whitaker, BBC Technology Correspondent*
